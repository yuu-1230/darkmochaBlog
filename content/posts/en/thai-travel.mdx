The summer of my first year at university, I took my first trip abroad. Thailand, and nobody with me.

It was my father who suggested going alone. The idea of being overseas by myself was a little frightening, but not having to sync plans with anyone — being able to go where I wanted at my own pace — was worth more to me than the fear.

I picked the wrong hotel on the first night. The next day I walked three hours round trip to reach a market. Later in the trip I spent about five hours on a train with seats made of what felt like plywood. And still, every time something went wrong, I worked out my next move and kept going.

The trip was in September 2024. It's been about a year and a half, but with the photos and notes I kept, here's a look back at my first time traveling alone.

## What the Nine Days Cost

The trip came to ¥146,000 in total. Looking back, that was pretty extravagant for a first-year student's first solo trip.

In September 2024 the rate was around ¥4.2 to the Thai baht (THB). Accommodation was cheaper than in Japan — with some searching you could find rooms from about ¥3,000 a night. To get around the city I used Grab taxis and trains like the BTS.

I spent my money on hotels and sightseeing this time, but if you stayed in dorms and planned your transport in advance, you could do this trip for considerably less.

## Day 1 — A Cheap Ticket to Thailand

### Narita to Suvarnabhumi

I flew AirJapan from Narita Airport to Suvarnabhumi International Airport.

If my notes are right, the round-trip ticket was ¥24,400. That still looks cheap to me now. That fare is what lowered the barrier to going abroad for the first time.

<ImageSlider images="/images/Articles/Thai/AirJapan.jpg" />

<Tip type="warning" title="I picked the wrong hotel on night one">
  The bathroom in the first hotel wasn't especially clean, and there were several stray dogs outside. When I booked it I had looked at nothing but the price — I should have checked the location, the interior, the reviews, and how safe the surrounding area was.

  I was happy the flight was cheap, but a hotel is not something to choose on price alone. On the very first day of the trip, I learned how much it matters that your room is somewhere you feel safe coming back to.
</Tip>

## Day 2 — "Three Hours Round Trip? I Can Walk That"

I walked from the P18 Hotel, where I was staying, to Chatuchak Weekend Market. I was confident about my stamina back then, and three hours of walking sounded manageable.

And I did manage it. The problem was that I traded the bus fare for the energy I was supposed to spend at the destination. These days I'd just take the train.

### A Market That Doesn't End

It's an enormous market that only opens on weekends, and the stalls simply keep going. I spent about three hours walking through it and never came close to feeling like I'd seen the whole thing.

The crowds and the noise come through better on video than in photos.

## Day 3 — Booking a Group Tour on a Solo Trip

On day three I joined a local tour: a floating market, Maeklong Railway Market, a coconut sugar farm, and the ruins of Ayutthaya, all in one day.

Traveling alone doesn't mean you have to arrange everything alone. The tour let me cover sights that were far apart without doing all the transport research myself. Where getting there looked difficult, leaning on a tour was a perfectly good option.

### The Floating Market, Seen from the Water

We took a boat along the canals, looking at the shops lining the banks. It's touristy, no question, but it's a scene you'd almost never encounter in Japan, and just being on the boat was enjoyable in itself.

<ImageSlider images="/images/Articles/Thai/Boat.png, /images/Articles/Thai/BoatInMarket.png" />

### Maeklong Railway Market, Rebuilt Every Time a Train Comes

The goods and the stalls come right up to the edge of the rails. The market is famous for the way the vendors pack everything away whenever a train arrives, but simply walking along the tracks and browsing was interesting enough on its own.

<ImageSlider images="/images/Articles/Thai/TrainMarket.png" />

### Ayutthaya Was Quieter Than the Photos

At Ayutthaya I got to see the Buddha head held in the roots of a tree. I'd seen it in pictures a number of times, but standing in front of it there was a strange stillness to it.

<ImageSlider images="/images/Articles/Thai/AyutthayaRuins2.png, /images/Articles/Thai/AyutthayaRuins.png" />

## Day 4 — Bangkok from Above, and a Hulk at Street Level

### King Power Mahanakhon

I went up to the observation deck that looks out over the whole city. There's a glass floor on the roof, and looking down through it makes your knees go weak.

The best part was the stretch between dusk and night. The daytime outlines dissolved bit by bit, and the lights of the roads and buildings brought the city back up out of the dark.

<ImageSlider images="/images/Articles/Thai/ThaiCity.png, /images/Articles/Thai/Mahanakhon.png" />

### Khaosan Road

After coming down from the tower I headed to Khaosan Road, the street people call the backpackers' holy ground. Nothing like the orderly threads of light I'd been looking at from above — down at street level it was all music and body heat.

The cheapest and best thing I ate on the whole trip, a plate of pad kra pao, was here. And in front of what I'm fairly sure was a cannabis shop stood, for reasons I never established, a giant Hulk.

When I try to picture Thailand at night, that Hulk still shows up alongside the skyline.

## Day 5 — Five Hours on a Hard Seat to a Hotel on the River

### Leaving Bangkok for the River

On day five I left the noise of Bangkok for a hotel on the river — about five hours each way by train. The seats were hard enough that you felt every joint in the track, and the journey alone was an endurance event.

<ImageSlider images="/images/Articles/Thai/Train.png, /images/Articles/Thai/Train2.png, /images/Articles/Thai/Train3.png" />

Part of me wanted to be there already, but I didn't mind watching the countryside go past the window. Five hours is a long time if all you want is to get somewhere efficiently. Watching the landscape change by degrees, though, was part of the trip too.

When I reached the hotel, the only thing I could hear was the river. I don't have many photos left from there, but compared to the Bangkok I'd been in the day before, even time seemed to move at a different speed.

### The Death Railway Wasn't Something I Could Just Enjoy

The Thai–Burma Railway was built during the Second World War under the former Japanese military. There are sections where you can walk along the track, and standing there knowing that history, it stopped being something I could take in as a tourist attraction and became complicated.

## Day 6 — The Three Great Temples and Asiatique

### Wat Arun Changes Depending on How Close You Stand

I took the ferry across in the morning and went around the temples. What stayed with me most was the decoration on Wat Arun. From a distance it reads as one large pattern; up close, it's thousands of small pieces of porcelain packed together.

Seeing the whole thing in a photograph and seeing the detail in front of you are almost two different buildings.

<ImageSlider images="/images/Articles/Thai/Temple2.png, /images/Articles/Thai/Temple3.png, /images/Articles/Thai/Temple.png" />

### Asiatique The Riverfront

In the evening I went to Asiatique. The shops run along the river, it's easy to walk, and it's easy to shop. The trip was nearly over, so I bought all my souvenirs there in one go.

<ImageSlider images="/images/Articles/Thai/AsianMarket.png, /images/Articles/Thai/AsianMarket2.png" />

## The End of the Trip — Thailand Back to Japan

Nine days sounds like a lot until you start, and then it's gone.

Before I left, I wasn't sure I'd really be all right on my own in another country. And in fact I did pick the wrong hotel, walk a distance I had no need to walk, and wear myself out on long journeys.

But what mattered on a solo trip turned out not to be doing everything by myself. Take a tour to the far-off sights, use Grab and the trains in the city, get a massage when you're worn out. Choosing my next move each time, on my own — that was what traveling alone actually meant.

Old houses sitting right next to high-rises; people and noise that never quite stop, even at night. Getting to walk a city like that on my own feet, and getting to keep making my own decisions along the way, is something I don't think I'd have found if I'd chosen anywhere other than Thailand for my first trip abroad.

The one thing I regret is that I filmed almost everything instead of photographing it. When I sat down to write this, there turned out to be surprisingly few usable stills. Apparently keeping a record isn't about volume — you have to think about how you'll want to use it later, while you're still shooting.

Next time I go abroad, I'm bringing a proper camera. And next time I'd like to travel a bit lighter, more like an actual backpacker.
